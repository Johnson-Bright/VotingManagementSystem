59`t[2] ABSTRACT

Despite rapid growth of the construction sector and the implementation of occupational safety policies in Rwanda, injury rates remain high, especially in urban centers such as Kigali City.

126`t[2] CHAPTER I. INTRODUCTION
127`t[3] BACKGROUND
128`t[] Occupational injuries remain a global health and economic challenge. According to the International Labor Organization (ILO), nearly 2.93 million work-related deaths occur annually as of 2023, including fatal occupational accidents 14%, and fatal work-related diseases 86% (7,8). Additionally, These estimates imply that every day approximately 6,400 people die from occupational accidents or diseases and that 860,000 people are injured on the job (8). In 2023 alone, an estimated 395 million workers suffered non-fatal injuries that led to several days of absence from work (7). These statistics underscore the immense burden of occupational injuries globally, especially in labor-intensive and high-risk sectors such as construction.
129`t[] The construction industry is consistently ranked among the most hazardous occupational sectors worldwide (5). Construction workers are reported to be three to four times more likely to experience fatal injuries and twice as likely to suffer non-fatal injuries compared to workers in other sectors (9). While high-income countries have mitigated these risks through robust enforcement and advanced technologies, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to report disproportionately high rates of injuries and fatalities due to weak regulatory systems, limited safety resources, and underreporting (9–11). For instance, in 2023, high-income regions reported 4.2 fatalities and 3,240 accidents per 100,000 workers, whereas Sub-Saharan Africa recorded 21 fatalities and 16,012 accidents per 100,000 workers, and Asia recorded 21.5 fatalities and 16,434 accidents per 100,000 workers (12–14).
130`t[] The situation in Eastern Africa mirrors broader trends observed across Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in the construction and manual labor sectors, where occupational injuries remain a significant issue. In Uganda, the construction sector accounted for 13% of all occupational injuries in Kampala by 2003. By 2014, the sector exhibited a fatality rate of 92 per 100,000 workers and an injury rate of 4,248 per 100,000 workers (13,14) . Similarly, in Ethiopia, occupational injury prevalence among construction workers ranged from 32.6% to 84.7%, with factors such as manual labor and inadequate training being key contributors to these high rates (2,15). In Tanzania, the fatality rate in 2013 was 23.73 per 100,000 workers, reflecting systemic vulnerabilities in occupational safety across the region  (16). In Gambia, more than 56% of building construction workers reported experiencing work-related injuries, further underscoring the widespread nature of this problem (17). A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted across Africa found a high overall prevalence of occupational injuries, estimated at 57% (18). A separate meta-analysis focused on Ethiopia revealed a slightly lower but still alarming prevalence of 44.66% (15). These findings collectively highlight the significant and ongoing challenge of occupational injuries in Sub-Saharan Africa, with implications for workforce safety and the need for targeted interventions across the continent.
131`t[] In Rwanda, the construction industry has become one of the fastest-growing sectors, contributing approximately 7% to the national GDP and about 8% to national employment(19,20). However, it remains one of the most hazardous industries. A 2012 study reported 482 non-fatal accidents among just 130 workers over a 780 man-month period, with building projects showing higher accident rates than other civil engineering works (21). Common injuries included slips, falls, eye injuries, nail punctures, falling debris, crushes, and amputations. These incidents are often linked to long working hours, poor equipment maintenance, lack of protective gear, unsafe manual practices, low literacy, and weak supervision, among other factors (13,21).
132`t[] The City of Kigali, the capital and a hub of rapid urban development, has witnessed a boom in building construction (22). However, it continues to face recurring occupational injuries due to a range of preventable causes, including inadequate training, overcrowded workspaces, substandard materials, psychosocial stress, and insufficient lighting for night shifts (21,22). These challenges persist despite the Rwandan government's efforts, including the 2015 Occupational Safety and Health (OSH) regulations and the 2019–2024 OSH strategy, which were designed to improve workplace safety standards (23).
133`t[] While these policy initiatives mark significant progress, gaps remain in both implementation and compliance. Many construction practitioners still lack adequate knowledge of safety protocols, and enforcement mechanisms remain weak. Importantly, most existing literature in Rwanda addresses general occupational health and safety without providing a detailed analysis of the specific socio-demographic, behavioral, and environmental determinants of injuries in the construction sector (23–25).
134`t[] Therefore, this study seeks to assess the prevalence and identify the socio-demographic, behavioral, and work environment-related determinants associated with occupational injuries among building construction workers in Kigali City, Rwanda. The findings aim to contribute evidence that can inform more effective, context-specific safety interventions and injury prevention strategies tailored to the Rwandan construction sector.

169`t[3] DEFINITION AND OVERVIEW OF OCCUPATIONAL INJURIES
170`t[251] Occupational injuries are defined as any physical damage, illness, or fatality resulting from a work-related incident. In the construction sector, these commonly stem from falls, electrocution, being struck by objects, and equipment-related accidents (28). The industry’s inherently risky environment, characterized by dynamic job sites, manual labor, and heavy machinery, heightens the vulnerability of its workforce. Injuries not only affect worker health but also lead to substantial social and economic losses due to disability, absenteeism, and treatment costs (2).
171`t[3] THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
172`t[] This study, “Prevalence and Risk Factors of Occupational Injuries Among Building Construction Workers in the City of Kigali,” is underpinned by the Domino Theory of Accident Causation developed by H.W. Heinrich. This classical model conceptualizes accidents as a sequence of interrelated events where the occurrence of injury is the outcome of a chain that begins with underlying social and personal factors. The theory identifies five key stages: (1) Ancestry and Social Environment, (2) Fault of Person, (3) Unsafe Act or Mechanical/Physical Hazard, (4) Accident, and (5) Injury (2,29)

175`t[] This study is guided by the Domino Theory of Accident Causation, which conceptualizes accidents as a sequence of events, each triggering the next from social environment and personal faults to unsafe conditions, accidents, and finally injury. The model highlights the importance of addressing both behavioral and environmental precursors to prevent injuries. Within the construction context, this theory supports interventions targeting the elimination of unsafe acts and the improvement of safety culture, training, and supervision. The relevance of this theory lies in its structured explanation of how both behavioral and environmental risk factors contribute to occupational injuries. In the context of building construction in Kigali City, where rapid urban development, informal labor practices, and limited regulatory enforcement prevail, each stage of the Domino Theory can be observed. For example, unsafe acts due to limited safety training or cultural norms (stage 2) and physical site hazards such as unstable scaffolding or lack of PPE (stage 3) are common precursors to accidents (29,30).
176`t[] By framing occupational injuries as preventable events within a causal chain, the Domino Theory emphasizes intervention at multiple levels. Addressing personal faults through training and awareness, and eliminating unsafe conditions through improved site management, can disrupt the sequence and reduce injury rates. This aligns with the study’s dual focus on understanding both the prevalence of injuries and the factors that contribute to them (30).

179`t[3] EMPIRICAL LITERATURE
180`t[] 2.4.1. Prevalence of occupational injuries among building construction workers
181`t[] The construction industry is globally recognized as a high-risk sector. According to the International Labor Organization (ILO), over 2.3 million people die each year from work-related causes, with construction contributing significantly to this burden(31). For every fatality, numerous non-fatal injuries occur, often resulting in long-term disability (4,5). The Engineering Safer Workplaces report, released in 2024 and based on 2023 data, reveals that nearly three million people die annually from work-related causes, while 395 million suffer non-fatal injuries, especially in high-risk sectors like agriculture and construction (32). Despite advancements in occupational safety in developed countries, construction injuries remain a persistent issue due to varying regulatory environments, especially in developing regions (5,33).
182`t[] In Sub-Saharan Africa, rapid urban development has fueled a surge in construction activity, yet this growth has not been matched by advancements in occupational safety (34). Studies report alarmingly high injury rates among construction workers, including 32.4% in Uganda (13), over 56% in The Gambia (17), and 64.1% in Ethiopia (9). Common causes of these injuries include night shifts, lack of training, inadequate use of personal protective equipment (PPE), poor enforcement of safety standards, and widespread informal employment. Risk factors such as job dissatisfaction, long working hours, alcohol consumption, and low income further compound these challenges(35). A regional meta-analysis also highlighted a pooled occupational injury prevalence of 53.23% among small-scale industry workers, emphasizing the significant burden of workplace injuries in the region (1).
183`t[] In Rwanda, particularly in Kigali City, the construction sector has expanded significantly, driven by rapid urbanization and government-led infrastructure development initiatives (36). However, this growth has come with a parallel rise in occupational hazards, underscoring a critical need for improved safety protocols (37). A study reported 482 non-fatal accidents among 130 workers over a 780 man-month period, highlighting the alarming rate of incidents in the sector. The majority of these accidents were attributed to lack of personal protective equipment (PPE), inadequate safety training, and unsafe working environments (21,37,38).
184`t[]  In the context of Rwanda, regulatory bodies such as the Rwanda Social Security Board (RSSB) and Rwanda Housing Authority (RHA) have acknowledged the systemic issues contributing to workplace accidents, including underreporting of incidents and insufficient inspections (39). According to Chapter II, Article 3 (points 1, 17, and 19) of Law N°40/2010 of 25/11/2010 establishing the Rwanda Housing Authority (RHA), the institution is mandated to act as the overall project manager on behalf of the State for all housing and construction-related projects. Additionally, RHA is responsible for designing and supervising the construction of all public buildings and preparing the budget for their construction and maintenance (40). However, enforcement of occupational health and safety (OHS) regulations remains a significant challenge, largely due to limited institutional capacity and the informal nature of a large portion of construction work (41).
185`t[] 2.4.2. Risk Factors Associated with Occupational Injuries
186`t[] 2.4.2.1 Socio-demographic factors 
187`t[] Numerous studies have documented the association between socio-demographic and economic factors and occupational injuries among building construction workers. These factors are critical in understanding the vulnerability of workers to injuries in high-risk environments.
188`t[] A study conducted in Nigeria revealed that younger workers were significantly more likely to sustain injuries, with those in the younger age group having 4.1 times the odds of experiencing occupational injuries compared to older workers (42). This finding highlights the higher vulnerability of younger individuals, potentially due to inexperience or engagement in riskier
189`t[] tasks. In Gambia, male workers were found to be at greater risk, with 3.06 times the odds of sustaining an injury, suggesting that gender plays a crucial role in injury occurrence, possibly due to physical demands or types of tasks performed. Additionally, workers who worked fewer than 8 hours per day had 3.46 times the odds of sustaining injuries, a result that seems counterintuitive(43). This could indicate that workers who have less time to complete their tasks may rush, increasing the likelihood of injury. Another study from Uganda found that daily income was a significant economic factor, with workers earning in or above the second quartile (USD ≥3.2) having 1.72 times the odds of experiencing injuries compared to those in lower income brackets (13). This could reflect the nature of the tasks performed by higher-income earners, who may take on more complex and hazardous work, or broader socio-economic factors that affect safety. Finally, research in Ethiopia showed that uneducated workers had 3.33 times the odds of experiencing an occupational injury compared to those with education (44,45). This highlights the protective role of education, as it may increase awareness of safety protocols and proper risk management, which can reduce injury risk.
190`t[] In conclusion, the socio-demographic and economic factors such as age, gender, income, and education are consistently associated with occupational injuries among building construction workers. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that address these specific risk factors to improve safety in the construction industry
193`t[] 2.4.2.2 Behavioral factors
194`t[] Numerous studies have extensively documented the link between behavioral factors and occupational injuries among building construction workers. In Gambia, smoking tobacco was found to significantly increase the risk of occupational injuries, with workers who smoked having 1.97 times the odds of sustaining an injury compared to those who did not smoke (17). This suggests that smoking may impair workers’ physical health and alertness, thereby increasing their vulnerability to accidents. On the other hand, alcohol consumption was found to be associated with a lower risk of injury, with an adjusted odd ratio of 0.27. This unexpected result could be due to confounding factors or the possibility that alcohol consumption might be lower among workers with more frequent exposure to injury-related tasks (17) 
195`t[] In Uganda, job dissatisfaction and job stress were identified as key behavioral risk factors for occupational injuries. Workers experiencing job dissatisfaction had 1.63 times the odds of sustaining injuries, highlighting the potential impact of workplace morale on safety outcomes. Similarly, job stress was associated with a higher likelihood of injury, with workers under job stress having 1.72 times the odds of experiencing an injury(13) . This suggests that workers under stress may have reduced attention, slower reaction times, or lower adherence to safety protocols, thereby increasing their risk. Conversely, routine use of personal protective equipment (PPE) was found to decrease the likelihood of injury, with workers regularly using PPE having 0.57 times the odds of sustaining an injury(13). This demonstrates the protective effect of PPE in mitigating occupational risks.
196`t[] In Ethiopia, similar associations were found between alcohol consumption and job stress with occupational injuries. Alcohol consumption was linked to an increased risk of injury, and job stress similarly contributed to higher injury odds, reinforcing the findings from Uganda. These results emphasize the importance of addressing behavioral factors such as alcohol use and stress in the construction industry to reduce injury risk (2,44).
197`t[] Finally, a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) further confirmed the association between occupational injuries and sleeping disturbances. Workers experiencing sleep disturbances were found to have a significantly higher risk of injury, likely due to fatigue and reduced alertness on the job (1).
198`t[] In conclusion, behavioral factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, job dissatisfaction, job stress, PPE use, and sleep disturbances play significant roles in the risk of occupational injuries among construction workers. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive safety programs that address both physical and behavioral aspects to improve worker safety in the construction industry.
199`t[] 2.4.2.3 Work environment-related factors
200`t[] Environmental factors play a significant role in the occurrence of occupational injuries (OIs) among building construction workers, as documented in various studies across different regions. In Uganda, workers in poor safety environments had 1.51 times the odds of sustaining an injury compared to those in safer conditions, highlighting the importance of a secure work environment in preventing accidents (13). In Nigeria, environmental hazards like substandard building materials and working at heights were major risk factors, with substandard materials increasing the likelihood of structural failures and falls, both common causes of injury(42). These findings emphasize the need for better quality control and safety protocols.
201`t[] In Ethiopia, the absence of health and safety training was a key factor contributing to higher injury risk. Workers without training had 2.05 times the odds of sustaining an injury compared to those who were trained. A meta-analysis from Ethiopia supported this, showing that workers without safety training had 1.63 times the odds of injury. The same study also indicated that regular supervision reduced injury risk, with supervised workers having 1.4 times lower odds of sustaining an injury. Additionally, a study in northwest Ethiopia confirmed that lack of safety training increased injury risk, with workers without training having 1.45 times the odds of being injured (2,15,44).
202`t[] A meta-analysis of studies from several African countries also revealed that workers with temporary employment or no safety training were at greater risk, with 2.13 times the odds of experiencing injuries(18) . This further highlights the importance of safety education and stable employment in reducing injury risk. Furthermore, a decade-long review across Sub-Saharan Africa found a strong association between occupational accidents and poor safety environments, indicating the widespread impact of inadequate safety measures and training (46).
203`t[] In conclusion, the studies underline the significant role of environmental factors such as poor safety conditions, substandard materials, working at heights, lack of safety training, and insufficient supervision in contributing to occupational injuries. Addressing these risks by improving safety standards, providing proper training, ensuring quality control of materials, and offering regular supervision is crucial to reducing injury rates and improving overall safety in the construction industry

238`t[2] CHAPTER III. METHODOLOGY
239`t[251] Study design and period
240`t[] This study adopted a descriptive cross-sectional design to assess the prevalence and determinants of occupational injuries among building construction workers in Kigali City, Rwanda. Quantitative data will be collected from construction workers during the period of August 2025 and September 2025.
241`t[251] Study area
242`t[] The study will be conducted in the City of Kigali, the capital city of Rwanda, located at the geographical heart of the country. The city occupies an area of 730 km² and has an annual urbanization growth rate of 4%. Kigali is composed of three districts: Gasabo, the largest district by geographical area (429.3 km²), followed by Kicukiro (166.7 km²) and Nyarugenge (134 km²). According to the fifth Rwanda Population and Housing Census (RPHC5), the population of Kigali City is 1,745,555. The rapid urbanization and ongoing infrastructural development in Kigali make it a key area for research on occupational health and safety in the construction industry (47).
243`t[251] Study population
244`t[] The study population consisted of building construction workers aged 18 years and above who are actively working in the construction industry in Kigali City during the study period. 
245`t[]    Sample size calculation
246`t[] The sample size will be 384 determined using the Kish and Leslie (1964) formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Z²PQ / D² ​, where Z is the standard normal deviate at a 95% confidence level (1.96), P is the estimated prevalence of occupational injuries (assumed to be 50% due to the lack of local data), Q=1−P, and D is the margin of error (set at 5%). Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size is calculated to be 384 respondents. To account for a potential 10% non-response rate, the sample size was adjusted accordingly.
247`t[] Sampling procedure
248`t[] A simple random sampling approach with proportional allocation was employed. A complete list of all active construction sites classified under Category 3 and Category 4 will be obtained from the Rwanda Housing Authority (RHA). Category 3 includes multi-store buildings (typically starting at G+2) designed to accommodate more than 100 occupants, such as apartment complexes, office buildings, and commercial structures. Category 4 comprises large-scale industrial, hazardous, or public-use facilities with a capacity to host more than 500 people, including factories, hospitals, educational institutions, and other publicly accessible buildings. These two categories were prioritized due to their scale, operational complexity, and the higher likelihood of occupational hazards, making them the most relevant settings for assessing the prevalence and risk factors associated with construction-related injuries  (48). From this citywide pool, six construction sites were randomly selected regardless of district to ensure feasibility while preserving representativeness across the main types of high-risk construction environments. Once the sites are selected, a list of all workers, including supervisors, will be obtained from the site foreman with approval from the site manager. Workers will be then randomly chosen in proportion to the total number of workers at each site to contribute to the overall sample size of 427 participants. If a selected worker declines participation, the next person on the list was invited to take part in the study.
249`t[251] Data collection tools and procedure
250`t[] Data will be collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire, which was include both closed-ended and semi-structured questions. Trained research assistants visited selected construction sites and interview workers in private and at convenient times. As per similar study conducted in Uganda(13) , The data collection tool will be cover the following areas: demographic information of the workers, history of occupational injuries sustained in the past 6 months that led to at least 6 hours off work, and factors influencing injury risk, including work environment, job satisfaction, job stress, safety measures, and risk-taking behaviors. 
252`t[] Variable measurements
253`t[85] The key variables to be assessed in this study included occupational injury prevalence, work environment factors, risk-taking behavior, job satisfaction, and job stress. Workers will be asked whether they have sustained any injury that resulted in at least six hours of absence from work within the past six months. In addition, safety measures, workplace supervision, and the general safety conditions of construction sites were evaluated using a validated Worker’s Response Device (WRD) questionnaire (49) , adapted to the Rwandan construction context.
254`t[85] Risk-taking behavior, which may increase the likelihood of occupational injury, were assessed using 11 WRD items. These include: Are you ready to take risks to increase your earnings? Do you go to an unsupported area knowingly? Does doing work at speed appeal to you? Do you quite enjoy taking risks? Do you take risks or behave unsafely to achieve other objectives? Would life with no danger in it be too dull for you? Would you enjoy fast walking underground? Are you rather cautious in unusual situations? *, Do you always wear safety items when working underground? *, Would you do almost anything for a dare? and Are you confident in assessing good and bad situations underground? *.
255`t[85] Job satisfaction was measured using 15 items adapted from the job dissatisfaction scale. The items include: Are you satisfied with the condition of your workplace? *, Are your co-worker’s co-operative and helpful? *, Do you think your job is respected by other persons? *, Is there any prospect in your job? *, Are the promotions of workers given with justice and honesty? *, Do you think you have got the right job for yourself? *, Does your officer appreciate your competency and quality? *, Do you think your work is difficult and arduous? Do most workers work from compulsion and fear? Do you think the workers get the right behavior which they expect from their officers? *, If you get an opportunity, would you like to leave this company and join another? Are you doing this job because you are getting sufficient salary in spite of disliking it? Do you think this work is a source of respect for a man like you? *, Are you allowed to make decisions independently regarding your job? *, and Are you happy with your family? *.
256`t[85] Job stress was evaluated using 14 items, including: Do you have to work hard in your mining job? Do you think your job is difficult and arduous? Do you think you have to work in a hurry due to an excess load of work? Do the other members of your family depend on you? Is the mining work boring and monotonous? Have you got a clear idea of your work and its results? *, Does your boss take care of your dignity? *, Do you have a salary adequate for your labor? *, Is your work full of danger? Does a co-worker try to defame you? Do you feel you have responsibility for the development of the mine? Do your co-workers spontaneously help you to solve any problem in the mine? *, Do you feel your service is a burden to you? and Are you happy with the working situation of the mine? *.
257`t[85] Each question offered three possible responses: Yes, cannot say, and No, which are assigned scores of 3, 2, and 1, respectively. For negatively formulated items (indicated by an asterisk "*"), the scoring is reversed: Yes = 1, Cannot say = 2, and No = 3. This scoring method ensures that higher scores consistently reflect more positive or desirable outcomes across all constructs.
258`t[85] In addition, a modified Jenkins Sleep Scale was used to assess potential sleep disturbances that may impact work performance (50) . 
262`t[3] Study variables
263`t[] 3.8.1. Defining variables
264`t[] 1.Outcome Variable
265`t[] The outcome variable in this study was “Injury Occurrence at Work in the Past 6 Months." It was defined as a binary variable with two categories: Yes (indicating the worker has experienced an injury) and No (indicating the worker has not experienced an injury).
266`t[] Independent Variables
267`t[] 1. Socio-demographic Characteristics
268`t[] The socio-demographic characteristics included Age, which were categorized into three groups: 18-27 years, 28–37 years, 38–47 years and 48+ years. Sex was classified into two categories: Female and Male. Marital Status was categorized into three groups: Single, Married, and Separated/Widowed. The variable Highest Education Level were divided into four categories: None, Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary. Employment Terms were categorized as Contract or Temporary. Experience in Building Construction was classified into three groups: <1 year, 1–4 years, and >4 years. Working Hours were categorized into two groups: 8–10 hours and 11–12 hours. 
269`t[] 2. Work Environment Characteristics
270`t[] The work environment characteristics will include Work Designation, which were categorized into four roles: Carpenters, Casual Laborers, Masons, and Others. The variable Ever Had Health and Safety Training were binary variable, categorized as Yes (indicating the worker has received training) and No (indicating the worker has not received training). Trained from will indicate whether the training came from a Training Institute or the Workplace. Written Health and Safety Policy/Program were defined as Yes (if a written policy exists) or No (if it does not exist). On-Site Hazard Communication Measures were categorized as Yes (indicating measures are in place) or No (if measures are absent). Workplace Supervision were categorized into Good or Poor based on the quality of supervision. PPE Provision were categorized as Employer (if the employer provides PPE) or Worker (if the worker provides their own PPE). Poor Safety Environment were a binary variable defined as Yes (indicating the worker perceives a poor safety environment) and No (indicating the worker perceives the environment as safe).
271`t[] 3. Behavioral Factors
272`t[] Behavioral factors will include Alcohol Drinking, which should be categorized as No (indicating the worker does not drink alcohol) and Yes (indicating the worker drinks alcohol). Drink Alcohol Before Work should also defined as No (if the worker does not drink before work) and Yes (if the worker drinks before work). Drink Alcohol at Work were categorized as No (if the worker does not drink at work) and Yes (if the worker drinks alcohol at work). Cigarette Smoking were categorized as No (if the worker does not smoke) and Yes (if the worker smokes). Sleeping Disorder were categorized as No (if no disorder exists) and Yes (if the worker has a sleeping disorder). Job Dissatisfaction were categorized as No (if the worker is satisfied with their job) and Yes (if the worker is dissatisfied). Job Stress were defined as No (if the worker experiences no stress) and Yes (if the worker experiences job stress). Risk-Taking Behavior were binary variable defined as No (if the worker does not engage in risk-taking behavior) and Yes (if the worker engages in risk-taking behavior). Use of PPE were categorized by frequency as Always, Sometimes, and Never.

285`t[3] 3.9 Limitation of study
286`t[] This study may face several limitations. Selection bias could occur if workers who have sustained injuries and are absent during data collection will be excluded, potentially underestimating injury prevalence. Recall and reporting biases may arise due to inaccurate recollection of past injuries or reluctance to disclose them. Additionally, measurement bias could result from workers misinterpreting questions in the structured questionnaire. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. 
292`t[2] CHAPTER IV RESULTS
293`t[] Prevalence of Occupational Injuries Among Building Construction Workers in Kigali City
294`t[] In this descriptive cross-sectional study conducted among 423 building construction workers in Kigali City, nearly half of the respondents reported experiencing an occupational injury within the past six months. The overall prevalence of occupational injuries was 49.2% (208/423), while 50.8% (215/423) reported no history of injury during the same period. This indicates a substantial burden of occupational injuries among construction workers in Kigali City at the time of the survey.
295`t[] Distribution of Occupational Injuries by Socio-Demographic Characteristics
296`t[] The occurrence of occupational injuries was described across various socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents. Although variations in injury prevalence were observed among different groups, no statistically significant associations were identified (all p-values > 0.05).
297`t[] Injury prevalence varied across age groups. Workers aged 38–47 years reported the highest prevalence (55.1%), followed by those aged 18–27 years (49.0%) and 28–37 years (48.3%). Workers aged above 47 years had the lowest reported prevalence (33.3%).
299`t[] By sex, injury prevalence was similar among males (50.0%) and females (47.3%). Regarding marital status, married workers had a prevalence of 50.6%, singles 49.5%, separated/widowed 47.4%, and cohabiting workers 42.5%.
300`t[] In terms of educational level, workers with no formal education had the highest prevalence (51.4%), followed by primary education (50.7%), tertiary education (50.0%), and secondary education (46.6%).
301`t[] Based on employment status, casual workers reported the highest injury prevalence (52.2%), followed by temporary workers (48.5%) and permanent workers (44.4%).
302`t[] With respect to construction experience, workers with 1–4 years of experience had a prevalence of 51.4%, those with more than four years had 50.0%, and those with less than one year reported 45.9%.
303`t[] Overall, while differences were observed across socio-demographic categories, the distribution suggests relatively comparable injury occurrence among most groups.
304`t[] Distribution of Occupational Injuries by Behavioral Characteristics
305`t[] The prevalence of occupational injuries was also described according to behavioral characteristics.
306`t[] Workers who reported alcohol consumption had a higher injury prevalence (62.3%) compared to non-drinkers (35.3%). Similarly, those who reported drinking alcohol before work had a notably higher prevalence (73.8%) compared to those who did not (51.5%).
308`t[] Smoking was associated with higher injury prevalence, with 64.6% of smokers reporting injuries compared to 46.8% of non-smokers. Workers who experienced trouble sleeping reported an injury prevalence of 62.7%, compared to 44.6% among those without sleep problems.
309`t[] Workers who reported experiencing work-related stress had a prevalence of 56.1%, while those without stress had a prevalence of 40.6%. Additionally, workers who engaged in risk-taking behaviors at work had a higher injury prevalence (62.8%) compared to those who did not (40.6%).
310`t[] These findings indicate noticeable differences in injury occurrence across behavioral categories in this cross-sectional assessment.
311`t[] Distribution of Occupational Injuries by Work Environment Characteristics
312`t[] Work environment-related characteristics were also described in relation to injury occurrence. Variations in injury prevalence were observed across categories; however, no statistically significant associations were identified (all p-values > 0.05).
313`t[] Workers who never used personal protective equipment (PPE) reported the highest injury prevalence (80.0%), compared to those who sometimes used PPE (47.7%) and those who always used PPE (49.2%).
314`t[] Workers who had received safety training reported an injury prevalence of 49.7%, while those without training reported 43.2%.
315`t[] Regarding daily working hours, injury prevalence was 44.4% among those working ≤8 hours, 51.3% among those working 9–10 hours, and 36.4% among those working more than 10 hours.
316`t[] Injury prevalence was similar across night shift categories: sometimes (48.6%), often (46.7%), and never (49.6%). Workers who reported the presence of a written safety policy at the site had an injury prevalence of 50.3%, compared to 35.7% among those reporting no policy.
317`t[] Similarly, injury prevalence was 50.1% where hazard signs were present and 34.8% where they were absent. With regard to supervision quality, injury prevalence was 50.0% among those reporting adequate supervision, 48.4% among those reporting inadequate supervision, and 33.3% among those reporting no supervision.
318`t[] By job role, carpenters had the highest injury prevalence (57.1%), followed by masons (53.4%), other roles (50.0%), and laborers (44.5%).
319`t[] Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated with Occupational Injuries
320`t[] In the adjusted binary logistic regression model, drinking alcohol before work remained the only factor independently associated with occupational injury.
321`t[] Workers who reported drinking alcohol before work had 2.14 times higher odds of sustaining an occupational injury compared to those who did not drink before work (AOR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.11–4.14, p = 0.024).
322`t[] Other behavioral variables, including alcohol consumption during work, smoking status, sleep problems, stress, and risk-taking behavior, did not retain statistical significance in the adjusted model.
326`t[2] CHAPTER V: DISCUSSION
327`t[] The prevalence of occupational injuries among building construction workers in Kigali City was found to be 49.2%, a considerably high figure that reflects the hazardous nature of the construction sector. According to Rwanda’s Occupational Safety and Health Profile, developed by the Ministry of Public Service and Labour, the construction industry accounted for 18% of all occupational hazard exposures, ranking second after mining and quarrying (51). This prevalence is notably higher than previous national reports, which documented increasing injury cases but did not report rates as elevated as those found in this study. Regionally, the findings are consistent with a systematic review conducted across Sub-Saharan Africa, which reported a pooled prevalence of occupational injuries in high-risk sectors, including construction, at approximately 57% (52). Globally, studies from countries such as India and Brazil have reported prevalence rates ranging from 30% to 50%, depending on factors such as industrialization, enforcement of safety regulations, and access to protective equipment (53). The 49.2% prevalence observed in Kigali aligns with these international trends, suggesting that construction workers in Rwanda face similar occupational risks to those in other low- and middle-income countries and underscoring the urgent need for strengthened occupational health and safety measures.
328`t[] This study further revealed that alcohol consumption before work was significantly associated with occupational injuries. Workers who reported drinking alcohol prior to starting their shifts were more likely to experience injuries compared to those who did not. This finding is consistent with research conducted in South Africa, where alcohol use before work was identified as a major contributor to workplace accidents in the construction sector (54). A systematic review by (55) examined alcohol and tobacco use among construction workers and found that this group is particularly vulnerable to hazardous substance consumption due to the nature of their work and social environment. The review highlighted that alcohol impairs motor skills, coordination, and decision-making, which significantly increases the risk of workplace accidents, especially in physically demanding and high-risk environments like construction (56). .
329`t[] Trouble sleeping was not significantly associated with occupational injuries in the adjusted model. This indicates that, after controlling for other behavioral and demographic factors, sleep disturbances did not independently predict injury risk. In contrast, studies conducted in high-income countries have demonstrated a significant relationship between poor sleep quality and workplace injuries (62). Research involving industrial and construction workers in the United States found that sleep deprivation and insomnia were strongly linked to increased accident rates, attributed to reduced alertness and impaired cognitive performance (63). The difference between these findings may be due to variations in how sleep disturbances are measured, cultural perceptions of sleep, and the nature of work environments. In this study, the lack of significance may also reflect underreporting of sleep problems or adaptation to irregular sleep patterns among workers, which could mask the true impact of sleep on occupational safety.
330`t[] Lastly, risk-taking behavior was not significantly associated with occupational injuries in the multivariable model. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Uganda, where self-reported risk-taking behavior did not significantly predict injury among construction workers after adjusting for confounding variables (64). Other studies across Africa have found no significant association between self-reported risk-taking behavior and occupational injuries. In Uganda, (65), reported that risk-taking did not significantly predict injury among construction workers after adjusting for confounding variables. Similarly, (66), in a review of occupational injury predictors across multiple sectors, found that risk-taking behavior was not a consistent factor in injury outcomes. (67) also noted that while risk-takers may be perceived positively in workplace settings, these perceptions do not necessarily correlate with increased injury risk. 
338`t[2] CHAPTER VI: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
339`t[3] Conclusion
340`t[] This study showed a high prevalence of occupational injuries among building construction workers in Kigali City, with nearly half of the respondents reporting injury within the preceding six months. Behavioral factors, particularly alcohol consumption before work, were identified as significant predictors of injury risk. Workers who consumed alcohol prior to starting work had more than twice the odds of sustaining an occupational injury compared to those who did not. While other behavioral factors such as smoking, sleep disturbances, work-related stress, and risk-taking behaviors showed associations in bivariate analyses, they were not independently significant after adjustment. 
341`t[3] Recommendations
342`t[] Reducing occupational injuries among construction workers in Kigali City requires a comprehensive, multi-stakeholder approach focused on behavioral risk factors. Policymakers should prioritize strengthening the enforcement of occupational health and safety regulations, with particular attention to behavioral hazards such as alcohol consumption before and during work hours. The development and implementation of national policies addressing substance use in the construction sector will provide clear standards for both employers and employees. Moreover, integrating behavioral risk assessments and targeted interventions into existing occupational health frameworks is essential to enhance preventive measures and ensure safer workplaces.
343`t[] Employers and industry stakeholders have a pivotal role in fostering a culture of safety by instituting regular workplace health promotion programs. These programs should emphasize reducing alcohol use, promoting smoking cessation, managing work-related stress, and discouraging risk-taking behaviors. Comprehensive safety training that highlights the dangers of alcohol consumption before work and raises awareness of behavioral risks should be routinely provided. 
344`t[] Finally, engaging workers directly in safety efforts is critical. Increasing awareness about the risks associated with alcohol use before work and other behavioral hazards, along with encouraging active participation in safety and health promotion initiatives, will empower workers to make safer choices and reduce their injury risk.
